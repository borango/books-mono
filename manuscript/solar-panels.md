## Solar Panels

They [catch](#pp02) [sunlight](#sunlight), an endless renewable resource, and turn it into electric energy.

Together with a modern charge controller, they stop charging when batteries are full and resume charging when in demand, which is an example of [self-regulation](#pp04).

A great opportunity on a boat, even better on a catamaran, is the available space for mounting solar panels. Your first choice is to install walkable, flexible, flush-mount solar panels on existing, horizontal surfaces: cabin roofs and deck. This follows the same logic as putting panels on roofs of houses at land. You want to [integrate, not segragate](#pp08). A solar farm on the ground looks way less natural than on roof tops.

How much solar panels you need on an Eco sailboat is a matter of your energy demand, which I discuss in thei [electro backbone](#electro-backbone) chapter. Together with the [wind turbine](#wind-turbine) and a recuperating [engine](#engine) or other "hydro generator"[^hydro_generator_misnomer], solar panels must deliver the supply for all of your electric energy demand.

[^hydro_generator_misnomer]: misnomer; this device does not generate water but generates electricty from motion through water

On land you can often scale up your solar panel installation to meet your preferred demand. With the limited space of your boat, you typically calclate backwards and scale down your habits until your reduced needs match the available supply of energy.

Therefore most likely you want to:

> **install all the solar panels that you possibly can**

Walkable solar panels are always flexible to a certain degree, which helps installing them on rounded structures. Even if your roof or deck is perfectly plane, and if you do not plan to walk on the panels, then the flexible ones are more robust against damage from things or people falling on them. 

Also the flexible ones typically have a bubbly surface which collects sunlight from all different angles, where the rigid panel with glass surface are designed to be oriented into an ideal angle - which is possible at land but not on a boat or vehicle.

The flush-mount connectors face downwards, into the structure where you mount the panel upon. This requires perhaps more work for the installation and eliminates the biggest vulnerability of each solar panel: exposing the connector box to weather and physical damage (like stepping on it). With the connectors hidden and well protected, there is little left on the outside that could break. After decades probably the plastic coat might degrade, and that's it. Flush-mount flexible panels are more difficult to source and more expensive than any other type. I would argue that after replacing one defective panel due to corrosion in its connector box, the flush-mount type alternative would have paid for itself. [^lost_many_panels_to_corrosion]

[^lost_many_panels_to_corrosion]: on my two sailboats I have lost a total of 5 flexible and 3 rigid solar panels to the single cause of corroded connectors in the connector box, probably in a combination of mechanical stress (stepping, waves) and exposure to saltwater (waves and spray)

Try to use as many panel units of exactly the same type as possible, rather than combining different types. The same type of panels you can combine in parallel (more Ampere) and even in series (more Volts) with the same charge controller. Otherwise you need to pair each panel with its own charge controller.

If you have a fixed bimini top installed, then this is the next best place [^bimini_next_best_place] for solar panels. In this case they should be rigid, because a soft bimini top does not provide enough structural strength to support flexible panels.

[^bimini_next_best_place]: Personally I don't like a bimini top above the helm, because it separates me from the main sail. It is in my way for reefing or dropping the sail.

I do not recommend to construct a new roof-like structure for the solar panels, for aerodynamic reasons. What seems to be horizontal at rest will be partially vertical when the boat is leaning or rolls in waves. I assume that you do not want an extra mizzen sail, and in particular not when winds are strong or gusty. It makes your boat more weather helm (increases its tendency to head up into the wind) and increases rudder force when you least need it. At the very least, it changes the balance in your sail plan and moves the wind's center of effort further aft.

If you already have a fixed bimini top, then the aerodynamic damage is already done and you can just as well add solar panels to it. I suggest that you do not add panels on top of your davits.

If you need to resolve the conflict between energy supply ("install all you can") and safety in a storm, I suggest that you keep the structure of your boat clean and safe for storms. In addition, use mobile or foldable solar panels in absolutely calm conditions. You can recharge your batteries and even sail in a calm with mobile panels that are strapped to the deck or to the reling.

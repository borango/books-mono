Want to be 100% autonomous and independent of external energy supplies on your sailboat?

Want to spend 365 days on anchor?

Then you can use my approach which I successfully applied to two sailboats. The steps are incremental: you do not need to do all of them at once. First steps already deliver advantages by themselves. Additional steps are building upon those results from earlier steps:

## 1. sail "South"

Eliminates the need for heating. On a small vehicle (car, sailboat) you cannot harvest enough renewable energy for heating your mobile home in a winter "North" of 40° latitude, e.g. Lisboa / Madrid / Napoli / Ankara.

Also paint and epoxy cure better in a warm environment (20° Celsius). Warmer latitudes give you more months per year for such tasks.

Plus, winter days are longer in the "South". Shortest day of the year e.g. in Denmark ~ 6 hours, versus Tarifa ~ 10 hours. 

*replace "South" and "North" if you are on the Southern hemisphere*


## 2. add solar panels

Fit as many solar panels as possible. Cover every flat or curved surface with flexible, walkable panels. Prefer panels of equal size and specs, then use different sizes in order to maximize harvest.

Do not install panels that can act as unwanted sails.

> your boat can now generate enough power for all smaller loads such as light, phones / tablet / laptop and for heating water


## 3. replace 12 Volt batteries

When your old batteries reach their end of life, replace with lithium ones. Prefer multiple, equal sized, smaller (e.g. 50 Ah) batteries over fewer large ones.

Expand capacity to satisfy _average_ (typical) daily 12 Volt consumption (excluding engine and cooking). For _above-average_ 12 V demand, see step #5.

Lithium batteries require less space and less weight than lead-acid batteries for the same capacity. You can use this advantage either for installing more capacity within the same space, or by saving weight at the same capacity. You could also match the old capacity in a first replacement phase, and add more 12 V lithium batteries later. 

If you have a main engine with electric starter, then plan battery models for the peak power draw of that. Also separate "service" batteries from "starter" batteries if you can't step down from 48 V yet.

> your boat can now generate and **store** enough energy for 24h loads, your fridge can run all night, and you can use the autopilot continuously on long passages


## 4. install wind turbine _(optional)_

Add a wind turbine, unless you can cover all your engery demand with solar, even in winter.

You can do this at any time, but I recommend to use all solar panels options first, to upgrade the 12 V batteries, and then observing your energy balance (supply versus demand) before installing a wind turbine.

Production from a wind turbine matches the energy demand on long passages: when you sail, there must be wind. When there is wind, you can produce energy.

> your boat can generate more power and energy in different weather (clouds) and at night, which is even more relevant during winter


## 5. add 48V lithium batteries

Store the bulk of your energy demand in 48 Volt lithium batteries, for various reasons:

1. heavy loads (> 1000 Watt) require much more massive wires over 12 Volt compared to 48 Volt
1. some heavy loads (e.g. engines) simply require a higher voltage than 12 Volt
1. large batteries (> 2 kWh) are commercially more available in 48 Volt format

Also in this step you need to add step-up and step-down conversion, to feed your 12 Volt subsystem from 48 V, and to charge the large batteries from 12 V producers (solar panels, wind turbine).

Upgrade 12 V batteries before you add 48 V, to save unnecessary conversions between 48 V and a weak 12 V subsystem.

Note that adding massive lithium battery capacity introduces a new type of fire hazard and the need for relevant detectors (heat, smoke), and fire extinguishers.

> your boat can now store and supply enough power and energy for every-day use, for cooking, and for mobility on engine; it is virtually impossible that essential loads (light, navigation equipment, VHF radio, autopilot) will draw your 12 Volt subsystem empty


## 6. add 2 kW inverter

Primarily to prepare for step #7.

Also useful to run other heavy loads such as power tools, or all the small chargers for devices that do not operate at 12 V or 48 V.

With 2 kW power you can also run electric heaters for a few hours (limited by battery capacity), possibly for drying clothes or for recovering after hypothermia.

While inverting 2 kW of power from 12 V is possible, it would require very heavy wiring for ~ 160 Ampere, versus 40 Ampere in the case of 48 Volt. Also the conversion efficiency is better in the 48 V case. Therefore, use a 48V -> 230V inverter.

With an abundance of energy on board of your Eco sailboat, you can even share your 230 V with other travelers. 

> your boat is now prepared to run any 230 Volt ("domestic") appliance, with heavier loads probably limited by battery to a few hours of continuous operation


## 7. replace gas-with-induction stove

Swapping out the gas stove and all gas bottles for an induction stove has many advantages:

1. saves a lot of space and weight (which can compensate the weight of your 48 V batteries)
1. eliminates the need for gas supply and exotic adapters around the world
1. eliminates flame-based cooking, fire hazard, and the need for fire extinguisher and fire blanket in the galley area
1. eliminates the risk of gas leaks, suffocating and explosion 


## 8. replace fossil-with-electric dinghy engine _(optional)_

At the end of life for your fossil-base dinghy engine, or at the next service interval, you can swap it out for a smaller ( 0.5 - 1 kW ) electric engine.

Ideally this electric engine could double as an auxiliary engine for your sailboat (easy if your sailboat is already powered by an outboard engine).

The dinghy use case can serve for gaining experience before you convert the main engine to an electric one in step #9.


## 9. replace fossil-with-electric main engine

In a quickly growing market you have more and more options for electric sailboat engines.

Transition is easy in case of an outboard model, and a bit of a planning and construction project in case of an inboard engine.

The toughest decision is the choice of sufficient engine power which can realistically be supplied by your batteries. You may need to settle for less engine power than you were used to.

Using an electric engine eliminates the need for fossil fuel on board and increases the reliability of your propulsion system. Electric engines are virtually maintenance-free and have much fewer part that can fail

Sizing batteries (step #5) and wiring depends on this choice in step #9. Of course you can add more 48 V batteries when you install the main electric engine. You should think through the full solution from the beginning. Then you can implement it step by step - in the course of a few weeks, months or even years. 


> **Your sailboat is now 100% self-sufficient in energy. You can plan voyages without stops for diesel, gas or shore power. You can enjoy long ocean passages or weeks on anchor without worrying about energy supply or mobility.**
